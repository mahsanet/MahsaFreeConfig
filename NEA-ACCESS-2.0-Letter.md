# Letter to NEA/AC — Continued Support for ACCESS 2.0

**Holistic Resilience**

July 2026

U.S. Department of State
Bureau of Near Eastern Affairs
Office of Assistance Coordination (NEA/AC)
Washington, D.C.

**Re: Continued support for the ACCESS 2.0 program — progress since submission, refinements to our approach, and new areas of work**

Dear Members of the Review Panel,

We are writing to ask for the Bureau's continued support of the ACCESS 2.0 program. The objectives of the original proposal remain unchanged, and events since its submission have confirmed them more clearly than we could have anticipated. What has changed is the scale of the opportunity, the maturity of the technology, and the size of the community in Iran that now depends on it.

## Background: what has changed since the last shutdowns

During the November 2019 and September 2022 shutdowns, the Iranian government was able to do what it intended: cut the country off. For days and weeks, almost no images, testimony, or reporting left Iran, and the outside world learned the scale of the violence only after the fact.

The recent shutdowns tell a different story. During the January protests and the shutdowns imposed during the June 2025 and February 2026 wars, information kept flowing out of Iran — footage reached international media, families stayed in contact, and journalists and human rights organizations continued their work. The difference was satellite internet, and a community of ordinary Iranians who had been equipped, before the crisis, to share it. That community exists in large part because of a program this Bureau funded.

## NasNet: what it is, and where it came from

Because members of the panel may not be familiar with it, we want to explain plainly what NasNet is and how it connects to ACCESS.

NasNet is a free tool, developed by our team, that allows the owner of a Starlink satellite terminal in Iran to safely share their connection with other people — not just those nearby, but users in other neighborhoods and cities, who connect to it securely across Iran's domestic network. It handles the difficult parts automatically: it configures the equipment, protects the identity of the terminal owner, and keeps the connection working safely even when the international internet is cut. In practical terms, it turns one satellite dish into a small, independent internet provider run by a private citizen.

NasNet was originally developed under the ACCESS program, **supported by NEA in 2023 in partnership with Lantern**. ACCESS 2.0 is the continuation and scaling of that same investment: the tool the Bureau seeded is now the backbone of a growing citizen-run network, and this program is how it reaches the general public.

## The numbers, and why they matter

- **Starlink terminals in Iran now number close to 100,000**, up from roughly 30,000 in late 2024 — growth that has accelerated after every shutdown.
- **NasNet has surpassed 5,000 active deployments.** Each deployment is a terminal owner sharing their connection with others.

The significance of sharing is arithmetic. One hundred thousand terminals, each serving a single household, reach perhaps a few hundred thousand people. The same terminals, each shared with dozens of users through NasNet, can reach **millions** — without a single additional dish entering the country. Sharing is the difference between satellite internet as a privilege for the few who can obtain hardware and satellite internet as public infrastructure.

The second-order consequence is strategic. Internet shutdowns work for the Iranian government because they are total: cut the cables and the country goes dark. Once a meaningful share of the population can reach the internet through channels the state does not control, **shutdowns stop achieving their purpose**. Information leaves the country anyway; coordination continues anyway. A tool that once reliably silenced the population becomes costly to the government — it damages the economy and inflames public anger — while no longer delivering the silence it was meant to buy. Scaling this network does not merely mitigate shutdowns; it removes the incentive to impose them.

## A force multiplier for the Bureau's other investments

This program also strengthens the broader internet freedom portfolio the U.S. government has already funded. Most circumvention and transparency tools assume some working connection to the outside world — which is precisely what disappears in a shutdown. A citizen-run satellite network restores that missing foundation:

- **Circumvention tools** such as Psiphon, including its peer-based Conduit model, regain working routes out of the country when domestic infrastructure is cut.
- **Peer-to-peer communication and file-sharing tools**, which depend on users being able to find and reach one another, can continue operating across the independent network during blackouts.
- **Internet measurement projects** such as OONI can keep monitoring and documenting the state of Iran's network from inside the country during shutdowns — exactly the moments when visibility matters most and is otherwise lost.

In short, this extension does not compete with other NEA-funded efforts; it provides the infrastructure that keeps them effective at the moment they are needed most.

## The human rights dimension

Behind the technical case is a simple human one. When information keeps flowing during a crackdown, abuses are documented rather than hidden, and the evidence reaches international bodies while it can still influence events. Protesters, journalists, women's rights defenders, and ethnic minority communities — those most often targeted when the country goes dark — retain a lifeline to the outside world and to each other. Families separated by the crisis stay in contact. And the knowledge that the world is watching has, in itself, a restraining effect on state violence. Every terminal shared through this network extends that protection to more people.

## How we are refining our approach

Our objectives stand; our approach to them has matured.

**Objective 1 — making the network accessible to the general public.**
Today, NasNet runs on one family of routers. To reach the scale this moment demands, we are extending it to the inexpensive consumer routers already common in Iranian homes and markets, with an installation process simple enough for non-technical users, and developing a management dashboard that runs on ordinary computers for those operating larger community deployments. Together, these changes turn NasNet from a tool for the technically committed into infrastructure the general public can install and run.

**Objective 2 — strengthening the community and building international support.**
A network of thousands of volunteer operators is only as strong as the support behind it. Under this objective we will:

- **Deepen partnerships with established digital security helpdesks** serving Iranian users, so that operators and distributors receive timely help with account and service issues before those problems take them offline. As an illustrative example, we are working with **ARTICLE 19** to strengthen and continue supporting the community of network engineers inside Iran that has grown around this effort — the people who keep the network running locally.
- **Expand our technical support capacity** to absorb the surge in demand that has followed each shutdown, so that thousands more Iranians can deploy NasNet and share their bandwidth.
- **Garner support from European partners and build international recognition** for community-run satellite access as a legitimate, proven model for shutdown resilience — broadening the base of support beyond a single funder and encouraging other organizations to invest in it.
- **Share what we have learned with groups in other countries facing similar shutdown scenarios.** Iran has become the proving ground for citizen-run satellite networks, but the playbook — the tools, the safety practices, the community model — applies wherever governments cut connectivity. We will build the capacity to document and transfer this knowledge, and to inspire and assist other groups building satellite-based shutdown solutions.

## Two new areas of work

Developments on the ground and in the satellite industry lead us to add two new areas to the program.

**1. Completing the physical layer of a shutdown-proof infrastructure.**
The remaining vulnerability in the satellite access model is the "last mile." Satellite dishes are expensive, visible, and easily targeted, and users must currently rely on domestic networks to reach them remotely. We have developed a concept for a low-cost, easily concealed mesh device that lets a single hidden satellite terminal safely serve dozens of nearby users — without depending on the government-controlled grid at all. Paired with NasNet, this completes a total infrastructure for shutdown-proof access: the connection, the distribution, the management tools, and the community to run them. We are prepared to move this from concept into engineering and field readiness within months.

**2. Preparing for direct-to-cell satellite service.**
Direct-to-cell technology — satellite connectivity delivered straight to ordinary phones — could eventually remove the hardware barrier entirely. We propose a focused effort to prepare for it: a feasibility study for Iran conducted with satellite and spectrum engineers and policy researchers, and sustained monitoring and advocacy around the international regulatory decisions that will shape whether this service can ever reach Iranian users, with particular attention to the ITU's World Radiocommunication Conference in 2027. The Iranian government will be organized and present in those proceedings; the internet freedom community must be as well.

## Closing

We respectfully ask NEA/AC to continue its support for ACCESS 2.0 and the program of work described here. The Bureau's early investment in ACCESS has already changed what a shutdown means in Iran: for the first time, the government cut the country off and the world kept watching. The technology is proven across thousands of live deployments, a second U.S.-supported funder has independently invested in its foundation, and close to one hundred thousand satellite terminals are now in the country waiting to be shared.

Windows like this one do not stay open. We are ready to move immediately, and we would welcome the opportunity to brief your team on the roadmap, budget, and timelines at your convenience.

Thank you for your continued commitment to the free flow of information for the people of Iran.
