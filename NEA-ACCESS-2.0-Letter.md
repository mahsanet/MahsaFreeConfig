# Letter to NEA/AC — Continued Support for ACCESS 2.0

**Holistic Resilience**

July 2026

U.S. Department of State
Bureau of Near Eastern Affairs
Office of Assistance Coordination (NEA/AC)
Washington, D.C.

**Re: Continued support for the ACCESS 2.0 program — progress since submission, refinements to our approach, and two new areas of work**

Dear Members of the Review Panel,

We are writing to ask for the Bureau's continued support of the ACCESS 2.0 program. The objectives of the original proposal remain unchanged, and events since its submission have confirmed them more clearly than we could have anticipated. What has changed is the scale of the opportunity, the maturity of the technology, and the size of the community in Iran that now depends on it.

## The objectives remain the same — and events have proven them right

ACCESS 2.0 was built around two objectives, both of which we are keeping intact:

1. **Build a secure, shutdown-resilient alternative internet network** for Iran, anchored in internet sources the government does not control — above all satellite internet — so that people can stay connected even when the state cuts access.
2. **Grow an open community** that can adopt, operate, and sustain this network safely over time.

Since the proposal was submitted, Iran has experienced two major nationwide internet shutdowns. Both confirmed the program's core premise: when the government severs connectivity at the infrastructure level, software-only circumvention tools stop working, while satellite-based, independently owned infrastructure keeps people online. Each shutdown has driven a visible surge in public demand for exactly what ACCESS provides — internet access that is independent, secure, and resistant to shutdowns, and that ordinary people can own and share with others. The question is no longer whether Iranians want this infrastructure; it is whether we can help them build it while the window is open.

## What has been accomplished since submission

The program has not stood still, and the progress speaks to both our capacity to deliver and the soundness of the approach:

- **Satellite internet use in Iran has grown dramatically.** Even conservative estimates showed a twenty-fold increase in Starlink use during 2024, and adoption has accelerated further after each shutdown. A meaningful base of terminals now exists inside the country. The limiting factor is no longer hardware — it is safe, simple ways for owners to share and manage their connections.

- **Our bandwidth-sharing platform, NasNet, now has more than 5,000 active deployments.** NasNet grew directly out of the ACCESS work. It allows a satellite terminal owner to securely share their connection with others, including users far from the terminal itself. With thousands of active deployments — each serving multiple households — this is no longer a pilot. It is a functioning, community-operated network layer inside Iran, precisely what ACCESS 2.0 set out to build.

- **A second U.S.-supported internet freedom funder has invested in the platform.** The Open Technology Fund is now supporting the development of the next generation of NasNet, which runs directly on affordable, widely available routers and manages them continuously and safely. This independent investment validates the technical direction of ACCESS 2.0 and means NEA support would build on a co-funded, de-risked foundation rather than starting from scratch.

## How we are refining our approach

Our objectives stand; our approach to them has matured. The next phase focuses on two priorities:

**1. Making the platform accessible to the general public.**
Today, NasNet runs on one family of routers. To reach the scale this moment demands, we are extending it to the inexpensive consumer routers already common in Iranian homes and markets, with an installation process simple enough for non-technical users, and developing a management dashboard that runs on ordinary computers for those operating larger community deployments. Together, these changes turn NasNet from a tool for the technically committed into infrastructure the general public can install and run.

**2. Strengthening the community and its support backbone.**
A network of thousands of volunteer operators is only as strong as the support behind it. We are deepening our existing collaboration with established digital security helpdesks serving Iranian users, so that distributors and operators get timely help with account and service issues before those problems take them offline. We are also expanding our technical support capacity to absorb the surge in demand that followed the recent shutdowns. With these pieces in place, we can realistically help thousands more Iranians deploy the platform and share their bandwidth — multiplying the impact of every terminal already in the country.

## Two new areas of work

Developments on the ground and in the satellite industry lead us to add two new areas to the program.

**1. Completing the physical layer of a shutdown-proof infrastructure.**
The remaining vulnerability in the satellite access model is the "last mile." Satellite dishes are expensive, visible, and easily targeted, and users must currently rely on domestic networks to reach them remotely. We have developed a concept for a low-cost, easily concealed mesh device that lets a single hidden satellite terminal safely serve dozens of nearby users — without depending on the government-controlled grid at all. Paired with NasNet, this completes a total infrastructure for shutdown-proof access: the connection, the distribution, the management tools, and the community to run them. We are prepared to move this from concept into engineering and field readiness within months.

**2. Preparing for direct-to-cell satellite service.**
Direct-to-cell technology — satellite connectivity delivered straight to ordinary phones — could eventually remove the hardware barrier entirely. We propose a focused effort to prepare for it: a feasibility study for Iran conducted with satellite and spectrum engineers and policy researchers, and sustained monitoring and advocacy around the international regulatory decisions that will shape whether this service can ever reach Iranian users, with particular attention to the ITU's World Radiocommunication Conference in 2027. The Iranian government will be organized and present in those proceedings; the internet freedom community must be as well.

## Closing

We respectfully ask NEA/AC to continue its support for ACCESS 2.0 and the program of work described here. The proposal's objectives have been validated by events. The technology has been proven across thousands of live deployments. A second U.S.-supported funder has independently invested in its foundation. And the Iranian public, after two major shutdowns, is actively seeking what this program delivers.

Windows like this one do not stay open. We are ready to move immediately, and we would welcome the opportunity to brief your team on the roadmap, budget, and timelines at your convenience.

Thank you for your continued commitment to the free flow of information for the people of Iran.

Respectfully,
